Angsila and its oysters

For generations, oyster farming has shaped Angsila's economy, culture and identity. The coastal town in Chonburi Province is Thailand's second-largest oyster-producing area, with about sixteen shucking houses. Many families depend on small-scale farming, shucking and selling seafood, and the work has been passed down from one generation to the next.

Many farms still use semi-traditional methods. Farmers grow oysters on nylon rope and concrete to raise their yields and stay competitive with larger industrial operations. Seasonal workers, many from Myanmar and Cambodia, help with farming and shucking.

The problem

The industry's growth has left the town with a waste problem. About 35 tonnes of shells come out of the shucking houses every week. With no collection or processing system, they get dumped in open piles next to businesses, roads, homes and the coastline. Many locals know the piles are a problem, but have no affordable alternative.

Leftover meat rots in the piles. It smells of ammonia and hydrogen sulfide, and draws flies and other pests. The nylon rope and concrete from farming are still attached, so they have to be separated before the shells can be cleaned and reused. That is also why an older market, selling whole shells as animal feed, fell apart.

Most places that reuse oyster shells, including in the United States, leave them to cure for 6 to 12 months first. Angsila doesn't have the open space to do that.

Our goal

We set out to design, with the people of Angsila, a cleaning and processing system that turns the shells into useful products, built around the town's needs and resources. By transforming shell waste into a viable resource, the project aims to preserve Angsila's oyster-farming culture and traditions while reducing the industry's burden on residents, workers and the surrounding environment.

That lines up with APEC's guidelines on reducing seafood waste, which name shell build-up as a problem and call for reusing it instead. We had three objectives:

  • Find the gaps in how shells are handled now, and what the town already has to work with.
  • Develop a way to process shells into raw materials that can scale.
  • Work out whether the products would sell, and how the system could grow.

Team and sponsors

The project was sponsored by the Angsila Town Municipality and Chulalongkorn University's Department of Materials Science. Four of us from WPI joined three students from Chulalongkorn's Bachelor of Science in Applied Chemistry (BSAC) program in Bangkok, after a term of research methods and project preparation at WPI.

Between us we covered robotics, mechanical, aerospace, biomedical and computer engineering, professional writing, industrial and cosmetic chemistry, management and entrepreneurship. We worked with Chulalongkorn's Departments of Materials Science, Microbiology and Marketing: materials science for the lab tests, microbiology for the odor treatments, and marketing for the business analysis.

Our Thai teammates translated, helped us understand the cultural side of shell waste, approach shucking-house workers and officials respectfully, and anticipate how our ideas would be received. They also became our friends.

We were advised by Dr. Elizabeth Ryder and Dr. Douglas Creed at WPI, and by Prof. Dr. Patchanita Thamyongkit, Prof. Dr. Supawan Tantayanon and Dr. Numpon Insin at Chulalongkorn.

Talking to the people involved

We visited shucking houses and shell piles, and interviewed 24 shuckers, with our Chulalongkorn teammates translating. We also met town officials and the village head. Small shucking houses produce 2 to 3 tonnes of shells on a working day and large ones 4 to 5.

Odor was the most immediate concern for everyone we talked to. Getting rid of the shells is the shucking house owners' job, the town's landfills are almost full, and scattered dumping sites are hard to monitor. The village head said they would organise the community if there was a clear plan to follow.

We mapped everyone involved: shuckers, farmers, the municipality, informal waste haulers, possible buyers, and how they connect. Then we drew the same map for how it could work.

A process that can start small

Being there changed how we saw the problem. We arrived thinking of the shells as material for new products. Seeing the size of the piles, and hearing from shuckers and residents, showed us that odor, space, contamination, cost and who is responsible decide whether any product matters at all. So we put cleaning and odor control first.

We staged the process using NASA's Technology Readiness Levels, so a single shucking house can start with simple tools and the town can grow toward a central plant. It works with the rope and concrete instead of asking farmers to change how they farm:

  • Collect shells at one central site chosen by the town.
  • Crush them roughly to free the nylon rope.
  • Sieve out the concrete, then stir the shells in water for an hour to wash off the meat.
  • Dry them for three to five days.
  • Grind them into powder of different coarseness.
  • Sell the powder, or press it into coasters, tiles and pavers.

Making and testing the products

We ran the whole process ourselves on shells from Angsila, in Chulalongkorn's labs. Then we made pavers, tiles and coasters with 0, 25, 50 and 75 percent of the mix replaced by ground shell. Coasters and tiles were pressed at 50 MPa in a hydraulic press and cured in air and water. Pavers were pressed in a steel mold.

We tested five samples at each shell content in three-point bending. Strength fell steadily as shell went up: about 10.3 MPa with no shell, 7.8 at 25 percent, 6.0 at 50 percent and 2.8 at 75 percent. Water absorption rose with shell content, which means the material gets more porous. That suits uses like permeable pavers. Together the two results let you pick a shell ratio for the strength and drainage each product needs.

Dealing with the smell

Because odor was the most urgent problem, we tested spray-on treatments that could help while a collection system is set up. Eight sealed tanks of shells were treated with combinations of calcium hydroxide, photosynthetic bacteria and effective microorganisms, with plain water as the control. After a week, 29 volunteers ranked them by smell without knowing which was which, and we measured each tank's pH to check for environmental side effects.

Some treatments did better on smell and others on pH. The biological treatments look promising, but need more controlled testing before anyone can be sure.

Testing the coasters in real cafes

We also wanted to know whether a product would hold up outside the lab. For about a week, two Power of Cups Cafe locations in Bangkok swapped their cork coasters for ours and used them in normal service with hot and cold drinks. Staff said they worked well and needed less washing than cork.

Posters in the cafes told customers about Angsila's shell problem and linked to a video about how the coasters are made, which got about 1,700 views that week. That suggests the same kind of display in Angsila's own cafes, restaurants and tourist spots could build interest in local shell products.

Thank you to Bix Tagon Suwannavej, owner of Power of Cups, for helping us run the trial.

Business analysis

Every market evaluation was done for Angsila specifically. We ran SWOT analyses, 4Ps analyses (product, price, place, promotion) and product positioning for each product. Pavers, tiles and agricultural feed would be sold business-to-business. Coasters and body scrubs would be sold to consumers, especially through tourism, where they can also tell Angsila's story.

We costed two products in detail. A 10 cm coaster that is 70 percent shell powder would cost about 15 baht to make and could sell for 25 to 50 baht. A paver that is 75 percent shell, made 500,000 a year, would earn about 2 to 4 baht each.

The most flexible plan is to sell shell powder first, because it can go into construction, agriculture and consumer products, so the town doesn't depend on one market. We proposed a phased approach to our sponsor: improve collection and start small-scale processing, then use pilot production and market tests to decide when to invest in bigger equipment and more products.

What's next

Seven weeks wasn't long enough to fully cure the pavers or to run the odor tests to a firm conclusion. A full system still needs a site, money, trained operators, regulatory approval and an organisation to run it. Two ideas we left for later: shell breakwaters and living shorelines, which use whole shells but are blocked by Thai law, and biosurfactants, which could clean shells well enough for higher-value cosmetic products.

The process itself isn't specific to Angsila. Any oyster-farming town could adapt it.

Recognition

The project was nominated for WPI's President's IQP Award and for the Forum on Education Abroad's Award for Academic Achievement Abroad.
